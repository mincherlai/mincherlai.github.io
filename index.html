02 What We Study

Research Focus

Three core research pillars, bridged by advanced characterisation techniques at the surface and interface level.

I.
Polaron & Lattice Dynamics in Soft Semiconductors

How charge carriers dress themselves in a soft, anharmonic lattice — large polarons, dynamic disorder, and the origin of band-like transport in lead-halide perovskites, from ultrafast spectroscopy to synchrotron-based electronic structure.

II.
THz Defect Fingerprinting & Sensing Devices

Reading defects through lattice vibrations — defect-sensitive THz phonon spectroscopy of OHP thin films and single crystals, translated into THz-wave sensing, modulation, and imaging devices beyond photovoltaics.

III.
Surface & Interface Degradation under Operando Conditions

Watching buried interfaces evolve in real time — operando synchrotron characterisation of interdiffusion, phase transitions, and chemical evolution at surfaces and heterointerfaces, from lead-free perovskites to battery electrodes.

Polarons in Lead-Halide Perovskites: What the Structural Evidence Does and Does Not Establish
Inhee Maeng, Young Mi Lee, Jinwoo Park, Seung Jae Oh, Min-Cherl Jung*
A critical review of the structural evidence for polarons in lead-halide perovskites — time-resolved lattice distortion, dimensionality-dependent carrier–lattice coupling, and the first direct test of defect tolerance — separating what polaron formation does and does not establish, with proposed experiments to clarify carrier behavior.
Detailed summary

The question. Carriers in lead-halide perovskites are widely pictured as large polarons, and the lattice distortion travelling with them is routinely invoked to explain defect tolerance — screening of the carrier from charged defects. This review asks what the structural evidence actually establishes.

Three findings that complicate the picture. (1) Time-resolved structural probes now measure the photoinduced lattice distortion directly — yet calculations show that thermal lattice motion alone can produce the effective-mass increase once read as a polaron signature, so a measured distortion is not by itself proof of a polaron. (2) Carrier–lattice coupling strengthens from 3D to 2D, but the Ruddlesden–Popper and Dion–Jacobson families couple through different channels; their contrasting behaviour is set by which channel dominates, not by lattice stiffness alone. (3) The first direct test of defect tolerance does not support polaron screening — its authors credit the soft, disordered lattice — and new transport data show screening reduces carrier–carrier scattering, not capture by defects.

The bottom line. Even if polarons form, that alone does not make them the cause of defect tolerance. Settling it requires measuring the distortion as a function of excitation density, from solar-cell operating densities up to ultrafast-experiment regimes — the experiments this review proposes.

Band-like Carriers in a Soft, Anharmonic Lattice: Lead-Halide Perovskites
Young Mi Lee, Inhee Maeng, Jinwoo Park, Seung-Jae Oh, Min-Cherl Jung*
A review resolving the dichotomy of band-like transport in a soft, anharmonic lattice — from the Pb 6s² lone pair to large polarons, defect tolerance and the T−3/2 mobility law — with seven open questions and the measurements needed to settle them.
Detailed summary

The puzzle. Lead-halide perovskites carry charge like conventional band semiconductors — light carrier masses, steep absorption onsets, micrometre-scale diffusion even at high defect densities — while their lattice behaves almost like a liquid: overdamped octahedral motions, quasielastic Raman central peaks, exceptionally low thermal conductivity.

One framework. The review traces both faces to a single microscopic origin: the soft, strongly anharmonic, polar [PbX3]− framework shaped by the Pb 6s2 lone pair. The same chemistry sets the antibonding character of the band edges, the magnitude and multiple timescales of the dielectric response, and the slow relaxational dynamics that dress every carrier. The nominally cubic phase is treated not as a single geometry but as a thermally fluctuating ensemble of locally symmetry-broken configurations — so the lattice is developed before the electronic structure, inverting the conventional order.

Coverage and contested ground. Excitons, Fröhlich large polarons in the intermediate-coupling regime, carrier transport, defect tolerance, dimensional reduction to 2D and nanocrystalline derivatives, and symmetry breaking. Three contested issues — defect tolerance, ferroelectricity, and the interpretation of the T−3/2 mobility law — are assessed critically, closing with seven open questions and the measurements needed to resolve them.
